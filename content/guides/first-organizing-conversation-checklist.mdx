Your first conversation does not need to produce a union supporter. It needs to answer three smaller questions: Is this person experiencing the issue? Do they see it as shared? Will they take one low-risk next step?

## Before the conversation

- Choose one coworker you know well enough to approach, not simply the loudest person in a public channel.
- Pick a concrete issue: a changed promotion rubric, an impossible on-call rotation, inconsistent severance, or a new monitoring tool.
- Use a personal phone, personal email, or an in-person setting away from company systems.
- Decide on one possible next step before you begin.

Do not memorize a pitch. Bring a real question.

## A useful five-part shape

### 1. Open with what changed

“The new on-call schedule has me covering twice as many nights. Has it changed for you too?”

### 2. Let them describe their experience

Ask follow-ups before offering your theory:

- “What has that meant for your week?”
- “Did anyone ask your team before changing it?”
- “What explanation did your manager give?”

Listen for facts, intensity, and risk. A coworker who agrees that a policy is bad may still be unwilling to act—and that distinction matters.

### 3. Make the problem collective

If the experience is shared, name that carefully: “I have heard the same thing from two other people. It sounds less like an individual scheduling problem and more like how the rotation is being run.”

Do not reveal names or private conversations without permission.

### 4. Ask what a fair outcome would be

Move from complaint to remedy: “What would have to change for this to be sustainable?” Their answer may reveal whether the issue can unite people or whether different groups need different solutions.

### 5. End with a specific, proportionate ask

Examples:

- meet again for twenty minutes off work systems
- speak with one trusted coworker and report back
- compare the old and new policy
- read one relevant guide together

Ask directly: “Would you be willing to do that by Friday?” A warm reaction is not a commitment until the person agrees to an action.

## Afterward, write a minimal note

On a personal system, record the issue, the person’s level of concern, what they agreed to do, and when you will follow up. Do not write character judgments or collect intimate personal details. You are building continuity, not a secret personnel file.

## Signs to slow down

- They seem frightened, confused about why you are asking, or eager to end the conversation.
- You are doing almost all the talking.
- The discussion drifts toward rumors, revenge, sabotage, or disclosing sensitive data.
- You feel tempted to add them immediately to a large group.

You can close without pressure: “Thanks for talking. I won’t share your name or add you to anything. If you want to revisit it, reach me on my personal number.”

## What success looks like

A good first conversation leaves both people clearer than before. You know more about the issue, the coworker knows they are not alone, and the next step is small enough to complete. That is more valuable than a dramatic conversation with no follow-through.
