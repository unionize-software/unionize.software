Anger can create urgency without creating judgment. This checklist is for the moment when a coworker says, “We should dump everything online,” “Let’s add the whole team to a chat,” or “I can pull the data before they lock us out.” Slow the moment down. A legitimate workplace problem does not become stronger when workers hand management a separate conduct issue.

## Keep organizing off company systems

- Do not use company email, chat, tickets, calendars, repositories, or devices when a personal alternative is available.
- Do not assume a “private” workplace channel is private from administrators, retention tools, or legal review.
- Do not leave a coworker list, meeting notes, or draft demands in a work account.

Move the next conversation to a personal phone, personal email, or an in-person setting away from company systems. Keep the first group small enough that everyone knows who is present and why.

## Do not manufacture evidence or disruption

- Do not sabotage systems, poison data, falsify work, bypass access controls, or interfere with production.
- Do not scrape, download, or forward material simply because your account can technically reach it.
- Do not make threats, impersonate other people, or speculate publicly about facts you cannot verify.

Write down what you personally observed. Preserve material you are lawfully allowed to keep. If evidence collection raises access, confidentiality, or trade-secret questions, get qualified advice before acting.

## Do not expose people before they consent

- Do not add someone to an organizing chat without asking.
- Do not name a sympathetic coworker to prove that “everyone is on board.”
- Do not assign a public role to the most outspoken person by default.
- Do not assume contractors, vendors, team leads, and managers all have the same legal status or practical risk.

Support is not the same as consent to be visible. Ask directly: “Are you comfortable with me telling anyone else that we spoke?”

## Do not let speed substitute for structure

A viral post, a packed first meeting, or an angry petition can feel like momentum. None tells you whether people will take a second step when management responds.

Before a public move, workers should know:

- who has had a real one-to-one conversation
- which issues are broadly felt and which are team-specific
- who can reach remote, contract, night-shift, and less socially connected coworkers
- what the next decision is, who makes it, and how disagreement will be handled

## In remote and hybrid workplaces

Do not let the office become the whole map. Quiet remote coworkers may be cautious, isolated, time-zone constrained, or excluded from informal conversations; silence is not a position. Use deliberate one-to-one outreach instead of a surprise mass chat.

## If a plan feels reckless, say so plainly

Try: “I agree the issue is serious. I don’t want us to create a second problem that makes it easier to discipline people. Can we move this off work systems, confirm the facts, and decide who actually needs to be involved in the next step?”

The goal is not perfect caution. It is controlled, collective action in which workers understand the risk they are taking and have chosen it together.
