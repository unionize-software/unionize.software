When a policy changes, a layoff lands, or a manager’s story starts shifting, memory becomes contested quickly. Preserve a small, intelligible record—not a warehouse of company data.

## Start with a dated account

On a personal device, write down:

- when the event happened and when you learned about it
- who communicated the decision and in what setting
- the words you remember, clearly marked as exact or approximate
- what changed in your work, pay, schedule, access, evaluation, or reporting line
- who else appears affected
- what follow-up management promised

Separate observation from inference. “My access was disabled at 3:12 p.m.” is an observation. “They disabled it because I raised the issue” is an interpretation that may need more evidence.

## Preserve the comparison points

The most useful record often shows change over time or different treatment across similarly situated workers. Depending on the issue, that may include:

- current and prior policies or written announcements
- job descriptions, review criteria, schedules, pay statements, or benefits information that belong to you
- staffing levels, workload assignments, milestone changes, and on-call expectations
- dates of complaints, organizing conversations, discipline, role changes, or layoffs
- the names of relevant tools, dashboards, or programs without copying the underlying sensitive data

If coworkers received different explanations, each person should record what they were told in their own words. A clean set of independent notes is more useful than one document everyone has edited to match.

## Use a simple evidence log

| Date | What happened | Source | Who was affected | Follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| May 6 | New response-time target announced | Team meeting; personal notes | Support rotation | Ask for written policy |
| May 9 | Manager tied target to reviews | Direct message I received | Me; possibly team | Preserve message if lawful |

Record enough context that another person can understand the entry months later.

## Handle material carefully

- Use personal systems for personal notes.
- Keep only material you are lawfully allowed to possess.
- Do not bypass controls, scrape internal systems, or collect customer, patient, security, personnel, or trade-secret data.
- Do not forward sensitive company-wide material to a personal list merely to “save everything.”
- Limit access to people who genuinely need it; a giant shared archive creates its own risk.

If you are unsure whether you may retain a document, record that it exists and seek qualified advice before copying it.

## Before deciding what it means

Ask one trusted coworker what they observed. Then distinguish four questions:

1. **What changed?** Identify the decision, policy, or conduct precisely.
2. **How broad is it?** Find out whether this crosses teams, roles, locations, or employment categories.
3. **What is the timeline?** Note what happened before and after workers raised concerns or acted together.
4. **What remedy would matter?** Preservation should support a decision, not become an end in itself.

A disciplined record gives workers something sturdier than a screenshot pile: a timeline they can explain, compare, and bring to an organizer or lawyer if needed.
