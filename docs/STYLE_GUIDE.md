# Style Guide

This project should sound steady, specific, and useful. It should not sound like marketing copy, startup copy, or procedural sludge.

## Tone

- Write like a serious coworker who is trying to make the next move clearer.
- Prefer calm over urgency theater.
- Prefer directness over activist slogan-writing.
- Do not flatter the reader.
- Do not romanticize courage, risk, or sacrifice.

## What Good Pages Do

- explain what the page is for quickly
- distinguish the issue from surrounding noise
- tell workers what the page does not cover
- keep legal-adjacent claims narrow and dated
- point to the next page without turning into a link farm

## What to Avoid

- repeating the same idea three times in slightly different language
- writing about the site instead of helping the worker
- making every problem sound like an emergency
- implying certainty where the page only offers orientation
- vague praise like "powerful", "transformative", or "empowering" unless the page immediately proves it

## Claims and Sources

- Date any statistic, legal rule, or trend claim that could age.
- Use primary or near-primary sources when making evidence claims.
- Prefer links in context where the reader actually needs them, not a giant dump of links with no explanation.
- If a page relies mainly on practice and not formal sourcing, mark it `practice-based`.
- `mixed` and `source-backed` pages should usually declare structured `sources` metadata so the page rail can show the source footing clearly.
- Evidence pages should have either a structured source list, a body `Sources` section, or both.
- Legal-adjacent pages should stay educational, not advisory.
- Link to the hosting page, not a raw attachment, when possible.

## Source Handling

- Use the fewest sources that can actually support the page well.
- Prefer government agencies, statute text, official international standards, and direct research over commentary about commentary.
- Do not use a source just because it agrees with the page. Use it because it is the cleanest support for the claim.
- If a page needs a large pile of sources to stay upright, the page is probably trying to do too much.
- When a page mixes sourced footing with practical advice, keep the sourced part narrow and make the judgment calls explicit.

## Warnings

Warnings should protect workers, not scold them.

Bad:

> Never do this. It is irresponsible.

Better:

> Do not do this. It creates an easy disciplinary pretext and weakens the campaign.

## Page Rhythm

Most pages should follow a readable sequence:

1. What this page is for
2. Why the issue matters
3. What workers should look for or preserve
4. What not to do
5. What to read next

Checklists and reference pages can be shorter, but they should still make the boundary clear.

## House Voice

The voice is **calm, exact, worker-aligned, and unsentimental**.

Write to one worker who may be stressed, short on time, and unsure whom to trust. The page should make that person feel less isolated without making them less alert.

Every paragraph should do at least one job:

- orient the reader in a recognizable situation
- explain a mechanism or distinction
- help the reader compare facts
- support a decision or next move
- state a limit, uncertainty, or escalation point

If a paragraph does none of those things, cut it.

## Point of View

- Use `you` when giving a direct instruction or helping the reader make a decision.
- Use `workers` when the distinction between individual and collective action matters.
- Use specific institutional actors when possible: a manager, HR, legal, security, finance, an agency, or a bargaining committee. Do not use `management` as a substitute for describing who actually controls the decision.
- Treat coworkers as people with different information, incentives, authority, and exposure. Do not write as though a workplace is politically uniform.
- Do not present the project as the hero. The worker's judgment and relationships are the center of the page.

## Concrete Before Abstract

Prefer a recognizable workplace moment over a slogan or thesis sentence.

Less useful:

> Layoffs create confusion on purpose.

Better:

> A layoff can move faster than workers can compare what happened: a meeting appears, access disappears, and a severance deadline starts running before people know whether coworkers received the same terms.

After the concrete moment, explain the mechanism. Name what changed, who controls it, what evidence would confirm it, and why it matters collectively.

Use abstract terms such as `power`, `structure`, `leverage`, `discipline`, and `pattern` only after the page has shown what they mean in this workplace.

## Sentence Craft

- Vary sentence length deliberately. A short warning carries more weight when every sentence is not short.
- Prefer active constructions with visible actors: `HR changed the deadline`, not `the deadline was changed`.
- Keep one main idea per paragraph, but do not turn every idea into a one-sentence paragraph.
- Use parallel structure for real comparisons, not as decoration.
- Use contractions when they make the page sound more natural, except where precision would suffer.
- Read the page aloud. If every paragraph lands with the same cadence, revise the rhythm.

## Anti-Template Rules

The following constructions are not banned, but repeated use makes the corpus sound generated:

- `X is not Y. It is Z.`
- `That does not mean...`
- `The issue is...`
- `The point is...`
- `Two things can be true...`
- three consecutive sentences with the same grammatical shape
- an opening paragraph that states a sweeping thesis before showing a workplace reality
- an identical five-part heading sequence across unrelated guides

Use a contrast construction at most once on a page. If the same point can be made by describing a consequence, prefer the consequence.

## Richness Without Bloat

Richer writing comes from specificity and reasoning, not adjective count.

Add depth with:

- one or two representative scenarios, clearly labeled as examples
- the question a worker should ask before drawing a conclusion
- a counterexample or boundary case
- sample language for a difficult conversation
- the reason behind a warning
- the tradeoff between moving quickly and preserving trust or evidence
- a direct explanation of why a source supports the claim

Do not pad a checklist with scene-setting. Do not compress a legal distinction into a slogan.

## Distinguish Claim Types

Write so a reader can tell which kind of statement they are reading:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a named source and linked near the claim.
- **Legal orientation:** a narrow educational summary with jurisdiction, exclusions, and uncertainty visible.
- **Organizing practice:** experience-based guidance, described as practice rather than law.
- **Strategic judgment:** an interpretation whose reasoning is shown.
- **Illustrative scenario:** a plausible example, never presented as a reported event.

Do not slide from one category into another inside a paragraph without signaling the change.

## Editorial Quality Rubric

Score every substantial rewrite from 1 to 5 on each dimension:

1. **Specificity** — the page describes recognizable workplace facts and actors.
2. **Usefulness** — the reader can identify a decision, comparison, or next move.
3. **Accuracy** — claims stay inside the available evidence and jurisdiction.
4. **Traceability** — important factual and legal claims connect to appropriate sources.
5. **Human cadence** — the prose varies naturally and avoids canned rhetorical patterns.
6. **Safety** — following the page does not create avoidable exposure or false confidence.

A page cannot be marked editorially ready with a score below 4 in Accuracy, Traceability, or Safety. Automated checks can identify review targets; they cannot assign these scores on their own.
